THE FIRST SCRIPTS
Writing Systems
Two writing systems invented independently, 5,000 years ago, within 100 years of each other. Every word you read today descends from one of them.

Ancient Egyptian hieroglyphs — from the Greek hieroglyphika grammata (sacred carved letters) — were used for 3,500 years, from c. 3,100 BCE to 394 CE. The system combines three types of signs: logograms (one sign = one word), phonograms (signs for sounds), and determinatives (silent classifiers). The same sign can function as all three depending on context. Deciphered by Jean-François Champollion in 1822 using the Rosetta Stone.

CATEGORIES OF HIEROGLYPHIC SIGNS
LOGOGRAMS
One sign represents one word or morpheme directly. The sign looks like the thing it means. Example: 🎟 (sun disc) = rə (sun, also the god Ra).
PHONOGRAMS
Signs represent sounds — one consonant (uniliteral), two consonants (biliteral), or three consonants (triliteral). No vowels written — readers supplied them from context.
DETERMINATIVES
Silent signs placed at the end of a word to clarify its category. A seated man ጳ after any word tells the reader: this word refers to a man or his actions.
DIRECTION
Read in the direction the signs face. Animals and people face the start of the text. Can be written right-to-left, left-to-right, or top-to-bottom. Context determines direction.
UNILITERAL ALPHABET — THE 24 CORE SIGNS
Each sign represents a single consonant. Ancient Egyptian had no written vowels. This table is the backbone of the writing system.
BILITERAL SIGNS — TWO-CONSONANT SIGNS
These signs represent two consonants in sequence. Frequently combined with uniliteral phonetic complements that restate the consonants already written — a confirmation device, not redundancy.
KEY DETERMINATIVES
Determinatives are placed after words to indicate the semantic category. They are never pronounced.
HOW TO READ: CARTOUCHE EXAMPLE

A cartouche (Egyptian: shenu) is an oval loop enclosing the throne name and birth name of a pharaoh. The loop represents a rope tied at the bottom, symbolising the pharaoh ruling over everything the sun encircles. Champollion\'s ability to identify proper names in cartouches was the key to deciphering hieroglyphs.

THE THREE SCRIPTS OF EGYPT
HIEROGLYPHIC
c. 3100 BCE – 394 CE
Monumental script. Carved in stone on temples, tombs, and obelisks. Formal, complex, and slow to write. Each sign carefully rendered. Used for religious and royal purposes exclusively from c. 1000 BCE onward.
HIERATIC
c. 3200 BCE – 7th century CE
Cursive simplification of hieroglyphs. Written with reed brush on papyrus. Used for administrative documents, letters, medical texts, and literature. Signs recognisably derived from hieroglyphs but heavily abbreviated. Written right-to-left.
DEMOTIC
c. 650 BCE – 5th century CE
Further cursive simplification of Hieratic. Used for legal documents, commercial transactions, and personal letters. Far removed visually from the original hieroglyphs. One of the three scripts on the Rosetta Stone.
COPTIC
c. 200 CE – present (liturgical)
The final form of the Egyptian language. Written in Greek alphabet plus 6–7 extra signs for Egyptian sounds absent in Greek. The only form of Egyptian in which vowels are recorded — critically important for understanding how hieroglyphs were pronounced.

Cuneiform — from the Latin cuneus (wedge) — is the world\'s oldest writing system, invented in Sumer c. 3,200 BCE and used until c. 100 CE. It was pressed into wet clay tablets with a cut reed stylus, producing wedge-shaped impressions. Beginning as pictograms, it rapidly abstracted into phonetic signs. Used to write at least 15 different languages across 3,000 years. Deciphered by Henry Rawlinson in 1857 using the Behistun Inscription.

THE CUNEIFORM WRITING SYSTEM — HOW IT WORKS
LOGOGRAMS (SUMEROGRAMS)
A sign represents an entire word. 𒀭 (DINGIR) = god/divine. 𒆳 (KALAM/MAT) = land/country. 𒂗 (EN) = lord. Often used as ideograms in later Akkadian even when the Sumerian word was no longer spoken — like using & for "and".
SYLLABOGRAMS
Signs represent syllables (CV, VC, or CVC structure). The Akkadian syllabary has ~100 common syllabic signs. Example: 𒀀 (a), 𒀊 (ab), 𒀋 (ad), 𒀍 (ag). Signs can be combined: a+ba = aba. Sumerian used fewer syllabic combinations than Akkadian.
DETERMINATIVES
Silent classifiers, like Egyptian determinatives. 𒀭 (DINGIR) before a divine name = this is a god. 𒀳 (URU) before a place name = this is a city. 𒇽 (LU2) before a profession = this is a male person. Written but not pronounced.
PHONETIC COMPLEMENTS
Additional signs added to a logogram to clarify its pronunciation in context — the same device used in hieroglyphs. A sign may be read as a logogram in one context and a syllabogram in another. Context and convention determine which reading applies.

THE CLAY TABLET — MEDIUM OF RECORD
MATERIAL
River clay from the Tigris/Euphrates floodplain. Cheap, abundant, durable when fired. A clay tablet survives fire that destroys papyrus — ironically, fires that burned ancient archives often preserved the records within by baking the clay tablets hard.
TOOL
A cut reed stylus — triangular cross-section. Pressed into the clay at an angle to produce the characteristic wedge (cuneiform) impressions. Different angles and depths of the wedge create different signs. A skilled scribe could write ~100 signs per minute.
SCALE
~500,000 tablets survive in museum collections worldwide. An estimated 90%+ remain unread and unpublished. The Library of Ashurbanipal (Nineveh, c. 650 BCE) contained 30,000+ tablets — the world\'s first systematic library, organised by subject.
CONTENT
Grain accounts (oldest), beer rations, temple inventories, royal decrees, law codes, astronomical observations, mathematical tables, medical recipes, contracts, letters, dictionaries, poetry, mythology, and the Epic of Gilgamesh. Every aspect of Mesopotamian life recorded.

Writing did not spread — it was reinvented. Cuneiform and hieroglyphs were independent inventions. But from them (and a handful of other origins) every writing system on Earth either descends directly or was inspired by contact. This is the complete lineage from the first marks on clay to every alphabet in use today.

THE ROSETTA STONE & DECIPHERMENT
THE STONE
Discovered in 1799 by French soldiers during Napoleon\'s Egyptian campaign at Rashid (Rosetta). A granite stele inscribed with the same decree (196 BCE, Ptolemy V) in three scripts: hieroglyphics (top), Demotic (middle), Ancient Greek (bottom). The Greek was readable — providing the key to the others.
CHAMPOLLION
Jean-François Champollion (1790–1832), French scholar. On 14 September 1822, he identified that cartouches contained phonetically spelled royal names — proving the system was partly phonetic, not purely symbolic. He published his findings the same day. The date is considered the birth of Egyptology.
BEHISTUN INSCRIPTION
The cuneiform equivalent of the Rosetta Stone. Carved 520 BCE by Darius I on a 15m-high cliff in western Iran. The same text in three cuneiform scripts: Old Persian, Elamite, Babylonian. Henry Rawlinson copied and deciphered it 1835–1857. Old Persian was partially known — it provided the key to Babylonian cuneiform.
WHAT WAS LOST
Undeciphered scripts still withhold their knowledge: Linear A (Minoan, c. 1800–1450 BCE), the Indus Valley Script (c. 3300–1300 BCE), Proto-Sinaitic (the critical link between hieroglyphs and the alphabet, c. 1850 BCE), Rongorongo (Easter Island, c. 1200 CE), and several Mesoamerican scripts.
